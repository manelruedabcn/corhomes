ENERGY AND COMFORT

Heat recovery ventilation at home

Updated 26 November 2026 · 3 min read

The better insulated and more airtight a house is, the less air leaks through gaps. That is good for the bills, but it means solving something that used to happen by itself: renewing the air. Heat recovery ventilation does it continuously, without throwing energy out of the window.

The problem it solves

Breathing, cooking, showering or drying clothes produce humidity, CO2 and odours. In an old, leaky house this air renews itself, at the cost of losing a lot of energy. In a renovated house with good windows and airtightness, if it isn't ventilated properly, air quality worsens and condensation and mould appear.

Opening windows works, but in midwinter or midsummer it means losing the temperature that took so much effort to reach. And nobody opens the windows at three in the morning.

How it works

A balanced unit extracts stale air from the kitchen, bathrooms and utility room and supplies filtered outdoor air to bedrooms and the living room. The two airflows cross in a heat exchanger without mixing, and the outgoing air passes its heat, or its coolness in summer, to the incoming air. So the house is ventilated continuously while recovering a large share of the energy. The most efficient counterflow exchangers can recover around 80 to 90 % of that heat.

Benefits

  • Fresh air at all times, including at night and with windows closed.
  • Filtration: incoming air passes through filters that trap dust and much of the pollen, appreciated by people with allergies.
  • Less humidity and condensation, and less risk of mould.
  • Energy savings compared with ventilating by opening windows.
  • Quiet: no need to open up to ventilate on noisy streets.

Installing it in a renovation

In a full renovation it can be integrated well:

  • The unit goes in a cupboard, a suspended ceiling or a loft, with access for maintenance.
  • The ducts run through suspended ceilings, usually in hallways and bathrooms, to grilles in each room.
  • Two outside connections, one for intake and one for exhaust, kept apart.
  • A drain for the unit's condensate.

It has to be planned from the design stage, because it needs ceiling space and coordination with lighting and climate control. In the renovation plan it is one of the decisions best made early.

Decentralised units

When there is no room for ducts, there are individual units fitted in the façade of each room that alternate between supply and extract with a small heat store. They are less efficient than a centralised system but very useful in partial renovations.

In a Mediterranean climate

Here the heating savings are smaller than in northern Europe, but heat recovery ventilation still makes sense: it improves air quality, helps with coastal humidity and allows night cooling with bypass functions, which let outside air straight in when it is cooler than inside.

Maintenance

  • Replace or clean filters every few months, depending on use and surroundings.
  • Clean the heat exchanger periodically.
  • Check grilles and ducts.

It is simple but essential: a dirty filter reduces airflow and air quality.

Part of a whole

Heat recovery ventilation is one of the pieces of a near-passive house, together with insulation, windows and airtightness. It is also one of the pillars of wellbeing at home.
