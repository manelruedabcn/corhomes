Layered lighting: how to light every room

Updated 26 November 2026 · 3 min read

The most common mistake in home lighting is relying on a single fitting in the middle of the ceiling. It lights the room, but it flattens the space and leaves shadows exactly where you need light. The alternative is to think in layers, and to decide it before the walls are closed, while light points and sockets can still be moved.

The three layers

General light

It gives even illumination for moving around the room. It can come from well-spaced downlights, indirect ceiling light or ceiling fittings. It shouldn't be the only layer, nor the main one.

Task light

This is the light you need to do something specific: cooking, reading, working, putting on make-up. It goes where the task happens: under the kitchen wall units, at the bathroom mirror, on the desk, beside the sofa.

Ambient or accent light

It creates atmosphere and depth: indirect strips, wall lights, table lamps, light that picks out a textured wall, a shelf or an artwork. It is what makes a house feel welcoming at night.

Colour temperature

It is measured in kelvin (K). As a guide for homes:

  • 2700 to 3000 K: living rooms, bedrooms and rest areas. Warm, relaxing light.
  • 3000 to 3500 K: kitchens and bathrooms, where a little more clarity is needed without losing warmth.
  • 3500 to 4000 K: specific work areas, such as a home office.

Avoid mixing different temperatures in the same room, as it looks untidy. And avoid cold light of 5000 K or more at home: it is office or workshop light.

Colour rendering (CRI)

The colour rendering index shows how faithfully colours appear under that light. For homes, look for fittings with a CRI of 90 or more, especially in the kitchen, bathroom and work areas. Skin, food, wood and textiles look as they really are.

Room by room

Living room

Soft or indirect general light, floor and table lamps next to seats, and some accent light on shelves or walls. Dimmers on the main circuits.

Kitchen

Sufficient general light, task light under the wall units or spotlights aimed at the worktop, and a pendant over the table or island. Task light stops you working in your own shadow.

Bathroom

Front lighting at the mirror, at face height, to avoid shadows. General light suitable for wet areas and, if you like, a soft indirect light for night-time.

Bedroom

Dim general light, reading lights on each side of the bed with a reachable switch, and low ambient light. Two-way switches at the door and by the bed.

Home office

Side daylight if possible, good general light and a desk lamp or task light. There is more detail in home office design.

Hallways and stairs

Marker lights or low light with a presence sensor for night-time, without glare.

Circuits and control

Separate circuits let you create scenes: everything on for cleaning, only ambient light for dinner, only low light to watch a film. Dimmers multiply the possibilities. If you are considering home automation, leave the pre-installation in place during the renovation.

Common mistakes

  • A single central fitting per room.
  • Downlights in a grid across the whole ceiling, like an office.
  • Cold light in rest areas.
  • Ceiling spotlights shining straight into your eyes.
  • Forgetting sockets for floor and table lamps.
  • Deciding the lighting once the walls are already closed.

When to decide it

At the design stage, together with the furniture. That is what allows each light point to go where it makes sense. We include it in the renovation plan because moving it later means opening walls and ceilings.
