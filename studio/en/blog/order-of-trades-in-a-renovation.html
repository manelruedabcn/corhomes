PLANNING THE WORKS

The order of trades in a renovation and why it matters

Updated 26 November 2026 · 3 min read

A renovation is a chain: each trade needs the previous one to have finished properly. When the order fails, the two things that most prolong a job appear: waiting and work that has to be redone.

The usual sequence

Every job has its own particulars, but the general order of a home renovation is usually this:

  1. Protection and demolition: areas that aren't being touched are protected, and partitions, finishes and sanitaryware are removed.
  2. Setting out: the new layout and the water, power and climate points are marked on floors and walls.
  3. Structural building work: new partitions, reinforcements, new openings, screeds.
  4. Concealed services: plumbing, drainage, electrics, ventilation and climate ducts, pre-installations.
  5. Testing: pressure tests on pipes, checks on drains and circuits before anything is closed.
  6. Closing and levelling: filling chases, plastering, suspended ceilings, underfloor heating and screed if any.
  7. Waterproofing in showers and wet areas.
  8. Wall and floor finishes: tiling, flooring, stone.
  9. Joinery: windows if they are being replaced (better before the finishes), doors, wardrobes, kitchen.
  10. Fitting sanitaryware, taps, switches and light fittings.
  11. Painting and finishing details.
  12. Final clean, snagging and handover.

The rules that prevent problems

Test before closing

No pipe should be covered without a pressure test. No drain without checking its fall. Finding a leak after tiling means breaking what was already finished.

Messy work before delicate work

Windows go in before the final floors, and the final coat of paint comes last. A freshly laid parquet floor and a crew cutting brick in the same room are not a good combination.

Respect drying times

Plaster, mortar and screeds need their time. Painting over damp plaster or laying wood on a screed that hasn't dried guarantees stains, cracks or floors that lift.

Order slow items early

Kitchens, made-to-measure joinery, stone worktops or windows can take several weeks to make. They are measured and ordered at the start so they arrive when they are due, not when they are already needed.

Why trades get in each other's way

Without clear coordination, this is what usually happens:

  • The electrician arrives before the builder has finished the partitions.
  • The tiler waits because the plumbing hasn't been tested.
  • The painter has to come back three times because something is always missing.
  • The carpenter measures before the final floor is down, and then the doors don't fit.

Every return visit is a lost day and, often, an extra cost.

The schedule as a tool

A work schedule isn't just a list of dates. It shows what must be finished for the next stage to begin, who checks it and which materials must be on site that day. With it, the client can follow progress through clear milestones.

That coordination is the core of renovation project management: each trade comes in at the right moment and what was agreed is delivered.

And in a kitchen or a bathroom

In partial renovations the logic is the same, on a smaller scale. In a kitchen renovation the key is that services are designed for the final units before walls are closed. In a bathroom, it is not skipping the waterproofing.
