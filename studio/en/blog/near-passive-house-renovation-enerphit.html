ENERGY AND COMFORT

Near-passive house: renovating with Passivhaus principles

Updated 26 November 2026 · 3 min read

A passive house is one that needs very little energy to stay comfortable all year. The Passivhaus standard was created for new buildings, but there is a version for retrofits called EnerPHit. You don't need certification to benefit from the method: applying its principles in a renovation already changes completely how the house feels to live in.

What Passivhaus is

It is a building standard developed by the Passivhaus Institut in Germany. It sets very demanding limits. In new buildings, heating demand cannot exceed 15 kWh per square metre per year, and airtightness must be no more than 0.6 air changes per hour in the pressure test, known as n50.

EnerPHit, the version for renovations

Renovating an existing building has limits a new one doesn't: fixed orientation, walls that can't move, difficult junctions. That is why EnerPHit is somewhat more flexible. The permitted heating demand depends on the climate zone, and the required airtightness is 1.0 air changes per hour in the n50 test.

We talk about a near-passive house when these criteria are applied without necessarily reaching certification.

The five principles

1. Continuous insulation

Wrapping the house in enough insulation on façades, roof and ground-floor slab. It can be placed outside, the best option where possible, or inside if the façade can't be touched.

2. High-performance windows

Well-insulated frames, low-emissivity double or triple glazing and careful installation. We explain it in energy-efficient windows.

3. No thermal bridges

Thermal bridges are points where heat escapes more easily: slab edges, columns in the façade, shutter boxes, window-to-wall junctions. As well as losing energy, they create cold spots where condensation and mould appear.

4. Airtightness

Air leaks through gaps, sockets, service penetrations or poorly sealed junctions are one of the great energy losses. They are solved with a continuous airtight layer and checked with a pressure test, the blower door test, which measures how much air gets in.

5. Heat recovery ventilation

An airtight house needs controlled air renewal. Heat recovery ventilation does it while recovering most of the energy.

In a Mediterranean climate

Here the challenge isn't only winter. In summer, overheating must be avoided: exterior shading, control of solar gain through glass, thermal mass and night ventilation. A well-designed Mediterranean passive house is cool in summer and mild in winter with very little heating or cooling. We cover it in cooling a Mediterranean home.

What you notice when you live there

  • A stable, even temperature, with no cold walls or draughts.
  • Clean air and no condensation.
  • Much less outside noise.
  • Greatly reduced heating and cooling bills.
  • Smaller heating and cooling equipment.

When it is worth it

When you are planning a full renovation or work on the façade, roof or windows. That is when adding passive criteria has the lowest relative extra cost, because you are already working on those elements. If you are only redoing a kitchen, it doesn't make sense; if you are redoing the house, it is the opportunity.

Where to start

  1. An energy assessment of the home.
  2. Prioritise envelope improvements: roof, windows, façade.
  3. Design junctions to avoid thermal bridges.
  4. Plan the airtight layer and check it with a test.
  5. Integrate heat recovery ventilation and a small, efficient heating and cooling system, such as a heat pump.

Execution matters as much as design: a badly applied airtightness tape or interrupted insulation spoils the result. That is why project management and checks at each stage are essential.
