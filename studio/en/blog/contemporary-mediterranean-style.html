STYLE AND WELLBEING

Contemporary Mediterranean style in a renovation

Updated 26 November 2026 · 3 min read

The Mediterranean house isn't a catalogue of white arches and ceramic jugs. It is a way of building that grew out of the climate: walls that keep the cool in, filtered light, local materials and spaces that invite you to slow down. Bringing it into a modern renovation means keeping that logic, not the costume.

Light comes first

On the Mediterranean the light is intense, and the traditional house manages it: it lets it in during winter and filters it in summer. In a renovation that means well-oriented openings, shutters or lattices, linen curtains and pale walls that spread the brightness. We explain how to make the most of it in how to gain natural light in a renovation.

Materials that age well

Mediterranean style relies on honest materials that improve with use:

  • Lime: in plasters and paints it gives a matt, living texture, lets walls breathe and copes well with humidity.
  • Wood: in beams, windows, doors and furniture, ideally with oil finishes that show the grain.
  • Terracotta: clay floor tiles, ideal on the ground floor and in spaces that open to the outside.
  • Natural stone: for worktops, steps or shower trays; honed or tumbled finishes are less slippery and warmer.
  • Microcement or lime mortars for continuous, jointless surfaces that bring visual calm.
  • Natural fibres: linen, cotton, jute, rattan and esparto in textiles and accent pieces.

A warm, restrained palette

Off-whites, sands, earth tones, soft ochres, olive greens and the occasional deep blue as an accent. The key is restraint: few colours, repeated well, so the house reads as a whole.

Curves and soft shapes

Arches, rounded corners, niches and built-in benches belong to the Mediterranean vocabulary. Used sparingly, they soften a space and make it more welcoming. A niche in the bathroom or a long bench under a window can change a room without major works.

Fewer objects, better quality

The contemporary Mediterranean house breathes. A few pieces with a story are better than many decorative objects. Leave surfaces clear, build storage into masonry cupboards or bespoke joinery, and choose one or two pieces to take centre stage: a solid wood table, a fibre pendant, a handmade ceramic.

Inside and outside connected

Terraces, patios and porches are an extension of the house. Running the same floor, or one from the same family, between inside and outside makes the space feel larger. Large sliding doors, pergolas with reed screens or planting, and well-planned shade mean the outdoors really gets used.

How to avoid the cliché

  • Don't turn every wall into an arch. One well placed is worth more than five.
  • Avoid decorative ceramics everywhere; choose one focal point.
  • Steer clear of finishes that imitate materials: real wood and real stone age well; imitations don't always.
  • Combine tradition with modern lines: a kitchen with flat fronts and a stone worktop, a microcement bathroom with simple taps.

The part you don't see

A beautiful Mediterranean house also needs to be comfortable: well insulated, well ventilated and free of damp. Aesthetics don't compete with efficiency; they need it. If you are doing a deep renovation, see what it takes to get close to a near-passive house.
